Artist note
Antonis Mor, or Antonio Moro, was born in Utrecht, and studied under Jan van Scorel. According to Van Mander he went early to Rome. In 1547 he was in the Guild at Utrecht. His first important patron was Cardinal Granvelle, who took him to Brussels in 1548. Two years later Mor was again in Rome; thence he was sent to Madrid by Mary of Hungary, and on to Lisbon to paint Dona Maria, the Portuguese king's sister. For a while Mor passed about between Rome, Genoa, and Madrid, and in 1552 went into England to paint Queen Mary. He stayed perhaps a year, then returning to the Netherlands. He was now constantly employed by Philip II of Spain and by Margaret of Parma. Mor died in Antwerp in 1576. His best portraits are in the Prado, at Cassel, Vienna, and Brussels. There is a fine Alva by him in the Huntingdon Collection, New York. Such portraits prove him to have been a master not un- worthy of comparison with Holbein. See Henri Hymans: Antonio Moro. 191O.
Description
Half length, standing, facing slightly to the left. Dressed in black with brocaded dark crimson sleeves from the elbow to the wrist. Round her waist a heavy gold chain with circular fluted links. Two rings on each forefinger, one on the left forefinger a plain gold circlet. Two rings set with large stones on the third finger of the left hand, one on the little finger of the right; the right hand rests on a brown covered table in the left corner. She wears a white pleated yoke and is coiffee de la cornette. A large pearl drop is fastened inside her coif; a large ruby, the centre of a star of eight pearls, hangs on her breast. Dark brown background. The solid bars in the chain round the waist are decorated with fleur de lys, perhaps indicating that the wearer is of the French Royal House. In general appearance she resembles the Dona Fuana in the Prado, Madrid, more closely than any other portrait by Mor. The portrait in this Collection was formerly described as Queen Mary; it bears however no resemblance to her. A photograph of the picture, thus described was submitted to Henri Hymans, who rightly rejected the name of Queen Mary. Without examining the painting the eminent critic dismissed the attribution to Mor as well. In the opinion of the present writer M. Hymans would have reconsidered his verdict had he had an opportunity of judging the portrait by the original.
