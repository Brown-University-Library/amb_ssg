Artist note
Andrea d'Agnolo the Faultless (see Browning's Andrea Del Sarto) was born in Florence, and taught by Piero di Cosimo. He also studied the frescoes of Masaccio and Ghirlandaio, and the cartoons of Michelangelo and Leonardo. Then he entered in a partnership with Franciabigio, with him working in the Piazza del Grano. He also at this time decorated the Cortile of the Scalzi. In 1515-16 Andrea was invited to France by Francis I; he stayed there a year, and then returned ostensibly to fetch his wife Lucrezia and to buy pictures for Francis I. But through some laxity of moral fibre, some quality correspondent with his easy and effortless work, he fell to misappropriating Francis' money; spending it on his wife and her friends. Not of the strong stuff from which successful criminals are made, Andrea succumbed to remorse. His wife deserted him; he was driven to hack work, and at last died of plague in 1531. His works are in most of the great galleries.
Description
The Virgin, St. Elizabeth, and the Holy Children; four figures. Mary kneels on the left holding the Christ to her with her left hand. Her right lies on her knee. Her hood is deep rose shot with viridian-green; her bodice rose-red with golden undersleeves, her shawl bronze-grey, her skirt mossgreen. The Christ stands against her His right knee raised, His left hand on her breast. He looks round to the right at St. John, who stands profile left with crossed legs, holding up and pointing with his right hand. St. Elizabeth coiffed in white is behind him enfolding him with both hands; a pelt of copper-coloured fur round his waist. St. Elizabeth wears a dark plum-brown tunic under her white hood and shawl. The Christ has a gauze-like cambric cloth round His loins and held in His left hand. Painted after 1516. There are three known variants of this Holy Family. One in the Louvre with six figures; one at Munich with five figures and apparently the traces of a sixth head, and a repetition or copy of the Munich piece in Vienna. The example in the Annmary Brown Memorial differs in detail and in design from the Louvre and Munich pieces. It is closer to the Munich than to the Louvre example, but yet differs not only in having four instead of five figures, but also the following details. In the Providence example the Christ has a lawn drapery, in the Munich piece none; St. Elizabeth's hood is more fringed in the Munich picture and the folds at the back of her head are different. The Christ's head is tilted further back in the Munich example; the feet of St. John in the Munich piece differ in pose, his pelt is different, and in the Providence picture Mary has no lock of hair falling over her jaw on to her neck. Andrea painted numerous Holy Families; differing examples are in the Pitti, Florence, the Wallace Collection and the National Gallery, London, and in the Hermitage. The version in this Memorial was formerly in the Erle Drax, Olantigh Towers Collection. Sold at Christie's, 191O. Note to No. 41: Of this subject, Mary, the infant Jesus, St. Elizabeth and St. John by Del Sarto, there are known to be eight. That in the Pitti is possibly the first and probably the only one in which his wife does not seem to appear as having posed for the Mother of Jesus. The others, in which she is seen to have been the model for the Virgin, are in the Wallace Collection; Hermitage, St. Petersburg; Old Pinakothek, Munich; National Gallery, London; two in the Louvre, paris; and Annmary Brown memorial. In each the dominant motive is the Virgin Mary, Jesus, St. Elizabeth, and St. John. They are always in the centre foreground and never twice posed alike. For this reason it may be said of them that neither is a copy of another; but that all, to a certain extent, are original as to composition, and several vary as to number of attending figures. In official catalogues all, without reservation, are attributed to Del Sarto. R.C.H.
