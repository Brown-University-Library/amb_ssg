Artist note
Rubens was born at Siegen, in Westphalia, and first studied under Tobias Verhaagt and Adam van Noort. Later he went to Otho van Veen. In 1600 he went to Italy, there staying till 1608. Then he returned to Antwerp and became Court painter to the Regents of the Netherlands; in 1609 he married Isabella Brant. At this time Rubens developed an enormous business in Antwerp; his studio, or workshop, was filled with students and assistants (Van Dyck among them), who were ceaselessly engaged in carrying out his designs. In 1621 in Paris he was commissioned by Marie de Medici to paint a series of great canvases, celebrating her career, for the new palace of the Luxembourg. In 1628 he was sent to Madrid, where he met the young Velazquez. Next year another mission took Rubens to England, where he was knighted. His first wife had died, and in 1630 he married her niece, Helene Fourment, the sister of Susanne Fourment, whose portrait is the famous Chapean de Paille in the London Gallery. At this time he was fifty-three, Helene little more than a girl. His happy and prolific union gave Rubens a fresh impetus; his art responded to his own physical joie de vivre, and in the last years of his life he produced some of his masterpieces of sensuous colour and forms. Ele built a palatial house in Antwerp, and spent the summers in his Chateau de Steen. The victim of gout, he died in 1640. Rubens was a painter who immediately responded to his physical emotions. Thus his two periods of married life are marked in his art by work of ardently sensuous expressions, by a zest for violent action, for voluptuous form and colour. The sensual appeal of his work is so primal and candid as to be free from any meretricious flavour. But he cannot be ranked with the penetrative seers; he was too successful and too physical to be a revealer of immaterial things. On the other hand he is one of the supreme masters of design, draughtsmanship and paint. His influence in Italy, the Netherlands, France and in the best schools of to-day was and remains powerful. See Max Rooses: L'Oeuvre de Rubens. 5 vols. 1886-1892. (Translated by Harold Child, 2 vols.); Bode: Dutch and Flemish Painters. 1911
Description
The Virgin seated in the centre looking down to the right front, holding the Child with both hands. He stands on her deep blue mantle, on a parapet, looking down and extending his left hand to the child St. John, who sits on the right profile left, on St. Elizabeth's knee. He is quite naked; the child Christ has a fold of linen round His waist. St. Elizabeth is seated profile left on the right, dressed in a smoke-grey hood and mantle, with a gold coloured band over her forehead; in her lap, under St. John, is his furry pelt. The Virgin is dressed in a clear vermilion tunic, a blue-grey hood, and deep steel-blue mantle. Behind her on the left, looking over the Christ's head, stands St. Joseph, in a deep bottle-green mantle. The flesh tone of the Virgin's and St. Elizabeth's heads, and of the two children is conspicuously blond and luminous. The light shimmers on the little St. John's flaxen hair and gold-ivorine flesh. A remarkable rendering is made of refracted light beating up on the Virgin's face. A slightly differing version of this Holy Family, by Rubens, is in the Wallace Collection, London. It was painted in 1616-17 for the Archduke Albert Co-Regent of the Netherlands, and is alluded to in Max Rooses' great work on the master. Critical analysis of these two pictures proves that the Wallace Collection panel (same dimensions) is the earlier. The Providence picture shows that it originally had the necklace round the Christ's neck and the loose wisps of hair falling across Mary's forehead and cheek, that are seen in the London piece; but by an afterthought they were removed. Other differences occur, such as the size and tone of the Virgin's neck, which is larger and more disturbing in the Wallace Gallery picture; the fulness of the Christ's left cheekbone, in the London example, has been corrected in the panel in this Memorial, and the correction is clearly visible; the Christ in the Providence version has a little linen wrap over His waist; this may be a later addition, not by the master; the Wallace Christ has no drapery. The Wallace Collection St. Elizabeth's drapery is a warmer grey. In the Wallace Gallery example St. Joseph is stretching his left arm across the picture, behind the Blessed Virgin. As regards execution the London picture is crisper and more spontaneous in brushwork, as is natural in a first conception. The picture in this Collection came from the Sciarra Colonna Gallery in Rome.
